Research

How wide and deep are rivers across the CONUS?

Machine learning estimates of river width and depth under mean-flow and bankfull conditions for 2.7 million river reaches across CONUS, using USGS field observations with geospatial datasets (STATSGO, NHDPlusV2, NLCD, NID, EVI, D50, and aridity index). The dataset improves bankfull width and depth estimates by 30% and 78%, respectively, over traditional models.

At least one rating curve for every river in the CONUS!

Machine learning estimation and construction of synthetic rating curve power-law parameters for 2.7 million river reaches across CONUS, using USGS observations together with channel geometry and hydrological and geospatial datasets.

How fast does a riverbed move under supercritical flow?

Laboratory investigation of sediment aggradation and its propagation through river channels under supercritical flow, showing that aggradation waves travel much more slowly than hydrodynamic perturbations, typically at less than 5% of the initial flow velocity.

PaperDataset

Bringing missing river bathymetry into operational terrain-based flood mapping

A scalable low-cost geospatial algorithm, BARC-SS, that accounts for river bathymetry missing from DEMs by adjusting synthetic rating curves within the operational OWP HAND-FIM framework. Tested across six major CONUS rivers, the approach improves flood-mapping accuracy by up to 110% and flooded-building detection by up to 120%.

Does river shape change the routed hydrograph?

Updated NWM T-Route with CONUS-wide ML-derived channel geometry and evaluated how channel side slopes affect simulated peak flow, peak-flow timing, and runoff volume during major floods in the Arkansas-White-Red Basin. Sensitivity of peak flow and runoff volume generally increased with flood magnitude, while sensitivity of peak-flow timing decreased but became more variable.

Global river slope is not static!

A global dataset of temporal water-surface elevation (WSE) and water-surface slope (WSS) across 155,319 river reaches, derived from three years of Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) observations. The study shows substantial, spatially organized temporal variability in river WSS, with a global median relative WSS range of 0.44.

Where did Hurricane Harvey cause the most building damage, and why?

Machine learning estimates of flooded building damage across Texas and Louisiana following Hurricane Harvey (2017) at the ZIP-code scale, integrating NFIP flood-insurance claims with geospatial, hydrologic, and socioeconomic data. Feature-importance analysis showed that damage was highest where recorded flood depth and building value were greatest, particularly for two-story buildings.

Technical report

How high can Alabama’s rivers go each year?

Machine learning estimates of maximum of annual peak-flow discharge for ~78,000 river reaches across Alabama, using USGS streamflow observations with channel geometry, soil, drainage-area, dam, and other geospatial datasets. Estimated peak flows range from 0.0085 m³/s in the Lower Coosa River to 12,561.29 m³/s in the Tennessee River, with a median of 247.94 m³/s.

Technical reportDataset

Wildfire, erosion, and river recovery!

A field-based study of how a high-elevation Rocky Mountain stream (Colorado) responded to the Cameron Peak Fire in 2020 and subsequent erosion-mitigation efforts. Generated high-resolution DEMs from airborne LiDAR and UAV surveys, surveyed cross-sections using RTK-GNSS, and quantified longitudinal bed-elevation changes from 2013, 2023, and 2026. Results showed a shift from post-fire degradation to aggradation following erosion-mitigation measures.

When the flood map says “maybe”: accounting for roughness

A probabilistic flood inundation mapping framework using HEC-RAS simulations and the GLUE uncertainty method to quantify flood-map uncertainty under varying land-surface roughness along the Arkansas River near Conway, Arkansas. Flood extent increased with Manning's n, while the highest-probability zones (0.76-1.00) remained concentrated along riverbanks and low-lying floodplains.

Technical report

Not all rivers carry water the same way in the southeastern U.S.!

Geospatial analysis of nearly 700,000 streams across the southeastern U.S. showing that channel shape, not just channel size, is a first-order control on river conveyance. A triangular channel with 50% less cross-sectional area can convey roughly six times less water than a rectangular channel at the 2-year flood, with the effect diminishing rapidly downstream.

From landslide to flood: planning for the unexpected

An integrated assessment of hydrogeological hazards in the mountain area of Sondrio, Italy, evaluating a potential natural dam-break scenario associated with the Spriana landslide and modeling flood-wave propagation and sediment release through River2D and BASEMENT.

What happens when a bridge meets a river?

An assessment of how bridges, sills, and other engineered features control river hydraulics and flood behavior along the Parma River near Parma, Italy, using HEC-RAS hydraulic modeling. Bridges and sills acted as local hydraulic controls, with the Italia Bridge triggering a transition from subcritical to supercritical flow and a hydraulic jump.

About

“All models are wrong, but some are useful.” — George Box

This quote has stayed with me because it captures something I find fascinating about science: we try to understand a complicated world with tools that can never capture all of it.

A perfect example of such a complex system is a river system. A river can be reduced to lines, numbers, equations, and maps, but a real river is never just those things. It moves, changes, responds to its surroundings, and behaves differently from what we expect. That tension between the complexity of the real world and the simplicity of our representations is what keeps me curious.

And this curiosity has shaped the way I work. I bring together geospatial data, hydrology & hydraulics concepts, environmental modeling, and data science techniques to better understand rivers and floods. But I’m not only interested in what a model tells us. I want to understand what it leaves out, which assumptions shape the results, and what those limitations mean when we take the model into the real world.

For me, this matters because the things we model are not just numbers on a map. Floods affect homes, infrastructure, ecosystems, and communities. Better ways of understanding rivers and floods can lead to better information about risk and help people prepare for events that can have very real consequences.

That is the perspective I bring to my research, teaching, and the problems I choose to work on: not looking for a perfect representation of the world, but finding better ways to understand its complexity and making that understanding useful.
